## The Shallow Engineer Problem

Let's talk about these shallow engineers today.

There are many engineers who are nothing without AI. So let us dive into this.

First, I have noticed a common pattern among many open-source contributors during GSOC.

They can ship features.

They can write thousands of lines of code.

But they do not know what it actually does.

That is one of the major problems I see with these people.

**They ship a lot but understand very little about what and how things are going on.**

## Understanding Before Code

When I approach a feature, my first instinct is not code.

My first instinct is understanding who will use it.

-   What are the edge cases
-   How should I optimize it for future extensibility
-   Do we even need this feature
-   Do we have existing solutions that could be adapted

Many open-source contributors I have seen just jump straight into implementation.

They do not even check if the codebase already contains 80% of what they need.

Yet their pull requests end up with **1500 to 3000 lines of code** for a single feature.

_An engineer who truly understands the problem would probably need only 200 lines._

## The Vibe Coder

Another problem I see is their inability to make trade-off decisions.

There is a person I know.

He is not an engineer.

**Purely viber coder.**

When I told him he should understand trade-offs before starting any project, he went to ChatGPT and asked what is best for creating an app.

It said Flutter.

So he started building in Flutter.

When he wanted to create a to-do list app, he asked ChatGPT which framework to use for web.

It said Next.js.

So he started with Next.js.

When I saw him building, I knew this app would be heavily client-side.

It would have been better to use Vite plus React or a Tan stack instead.

Next.js is great but when you force it to be client-side instead of server-side, you deal with hydration errors and unnecessary complexity.

To-do list apps are mostly client-side.

They require minimal server interaction and would be much faster with simpler architectures.

He did not listen.

He started promptinh anyway.

By the way, that person has a **$200 Claude subscription**.

He is almost at peak usage as an engineer.

But that is not what we are discussing.

### Two Weeks of Wasted Effort

What I noticed was he spent two weeks building something that already existed.

He was literally copying _Superlist_, an open-source app, and _TickTick_.

He liked certain features from Superlist.

Certain features from TickTick.

He wanted to build something combining the UI of Superlist, the UX of TickTick, plus the habit tracking system from TickTick.

He had too much on his plate.

He wanted calendar scheduling too.

He purchased TickTick premium just to understand the features better.

He spent an entire two weeks hitting usage limits, **prompting like a zombie**.

The final outcome was terrible.

### The Code That Broke

The code was among the lowest quality I have seen.

When he showed me, I pointed out many mistakes.

**Whenever you are coding, you should be thinking about architecture first.**

As a non-engineer, he did not listen.

Instead, he asked Claude to fix the architecture.

_That is something you start with._

**It is the foundation of your home.**

_You cannot architect after building the structure._

He kept adding features until he reached a point where Claude could not help anymore.

The codebase was beyond his limit.

It was running on hope and prayers.

The code finally broke.

It was so broken that actions in the calendar did not reflect in todo.

Marking a task in the to-do list did not reflect in the calendar.

Connecting Google Calendar would not update properly.

When it did update, all other events disappeared.

He finally called me and said _this is really messed up, I cannot do anything_.

## The Complexity He Missed

I told he should not be building something that already exists.

**He do not understand the amount of complexity these products cover.**

There are things he cannot even think of.

A task having a subtask.

That subtask having its own subtask.

Sub nested structures.

In TickTick, there are multiple types of tasks.

-   Task
-   Checklist task
-   Nested sub-issues
-   Nested sub-tasks

On top of that, you can track Pomodoro.

You can track stopwatch.

Multiple counting methods for a task.

Then there are integrations.

-   Alexa
-   ChatGPT
-   Web app
-   Desktop app
-   Mobile app
-   Notification system

**Countless edge cases to cover for every single feature.**

He shipped many things but nothing turned out meaningful.

## What They Lack

Because he did not know how to architect.

How to build from the ground up.

**This is what happens when you reach the peak of AI dependency and realize you have wasted a great deal of time.**

These people are stuck because they cannot decide trade-offs properly.

They do not know how to scale products.

They do not know what maintainability means.

_Security is not even in their vocabulary._

Cost optimization?

I think we can already see the evidence.

He spent **$200** on a Claude subscription.

Instead, he could have purchased TickTick or Superlist premium for around **$10 to $20 for a year**.

## Decision Bugs

Then comes architectural decisions.

These people do not even use their brain.

**Most bugs today are not syntax bugs.**

**They are decision bugs.**

Code is not the commodity anymore.

_Code that compiles means nothing._

**Decision bugs are the bigger challenge.**

These Vibe coders do not even know the code they are shipping.

What it does.

What it means.

They do not know how debugging works.

They do not know what is breaking when something breaks.

I am not bashing on them.

_I am saying if it is not your field, you should not be trying it._

If they were using AI in the medical field, most patients would probably be dead by now.

### Symptoms of AI Dependency

A few more symptoms I have seen with these people.

They are unable to debug without AI.

They do not know what dependency hell means.

They just trust whatever AI writes.

They do not know what patches mean.

There are many fundamental things they do not understand.

**They just copy blindly and end up with half-baked projects that are not good enough for daily use or maintenance.**

## Fundamentals That Matter

Now let me talk about some fundamentals that are super important in the AI era.

1.  **networking** You should learn how networking works.
    
2.  **databases** You should know how databases work. Their limitations. How they handle load. How to optimize queries.
    
3.  **memory management** It may sound basic but you have to think in terms of resources.
    

Memory is not infinite.

You should optimize your codebase for the end user.

4.  **Asynchronous systems**

Caching.

Operating system.

Browser internals if you are building for the web.

### AI Amplifies What You Have

AI made implementation cheaper.

_But understanding is expensive._

**Good engineers with AI become 10x.**

**Weak engineers produce 10x more garbage.**

_AI amplifies whatever you have._

## Deploying Naked

Then comes the final problem.

Whenever these people somehow manage to build a successful product, they deploy without any monitoring.

Without logging.

No rate limiting.

No security rules.

Exposed APIs.

No backup.

No observability.

**They just deploy it on the internet.**

_It is like someone walking naked into a hostile environment where anyone can cause damage._

Earlier we had Tutorial hells or script kiddies in the hacking world.

They used to copy-paste things without building anything meaningful.

Now we have a replacement.

**The AI/Viber coder.**

**And they are much more dangerous.**

Because they do not know what they are doing.

They trust AI so much they do not even question it.

Whatever AI does, they do it.

They do not know how vulnerable they are.

A single slip-up and their whole security is gone.

Their whole operating system is exposed.

_Their code is literally open to the wild._

## What Survives

**The only fundamentals that survive in software engineering from the 1990s till now are the core concepts.**

Frameworks die.

Tools die.

Models change.

**But the core concepts always remain.**

TCP is still there.

Databases are still there.

Distributed systems work the same way.

Algorithms are still relevant.

Architecture thinking survives.

Systems built on solid architecture still thrive.

This is what will stay relevant.

Frameworks, tools, models will come and go.

_You should not depend on them._

## Maintenance Is the Real Challenge

**AI reduces effort but not complexity.**

Building apps becomes much easier.

_Maintaining them is not easier at all._

When it comes to maintaining, you need to think about:

-   current scale
-   how to deploy
-   how to scale
-   database migration strategies in case of failures
-   concurrency edge cases

The new engineering will revolve around understanding of business, systems, users, dependencies, and architectural decisions instead of giving AI full control of your thinking.

**A 10x engineer uses AI for features, not for architecture.**

In the industry, it used to be can you code.

Now it is shifting toward:

-   can you code with AI
-   and can you also build reliable systems

That is where we are with the current state of AI and the AI coder phenomenon.

I hope you learned some new things from this.
